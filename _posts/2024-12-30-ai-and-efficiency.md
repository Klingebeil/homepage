It’s never a good sign when buzzwords[^1] start to appear your companies strategy. In most cases the goal is not to actually provide the organization with solid guidance but to signal *innovativeness*: look we’re also doing this! 

A very timely contemporary example is a particular line of reasoning for the adoption of “*AI*” in journalism: it will make journalists more *efficient*.[^2]

Arguably this argument contains two buzzwords. *AI* is at best a very loose marketing term, labelling a number of different methods, tools and ideas. Arguably most people today understand this to mean *ChatGPT* or *Generative AI* but I remember the times in which *AI* simply referred to *machine learning* techniques.

But the thing I want to focus on here is *efficiency*.

Let's start with the obvious part: if you want to increase the efficiency of a newsroom, why would you start by adopting AI tools? Yes, this is a rather cheap rhetorical trick I am playing here but an important one, because what are we (or you) trying to achieve? Is the goal the adoption of AI-tools or the increasing of efficiency?

There are of course multiple different strategies for an increase of efficiency in an organization: better decision-making, increasing work speed, optimizing work-flows and — yes — adopting different tooling. But framing the question around *efficiency*, not *AI*-use, gives you already a very different menu of things to choose from to reach your goal.

## Old reasons, old myths

I do think most people using the "*AI* for efficiency gains"-argument are somewhat honest in this regard. The goal is not primarily an efficiency gain but *AI* adoption, efficiency is simply the broadest possible reason that can be given. There’s also a tinge of inevitability here: there’s no other option if you want to stay competitive, media companies **have** to adopt or risk being left behind.

David Noble, one of the best historians of automations in the 20th century, encountered a similar line of reasoning when it came to factory automation in the 70s and 80s:

> **Managers feel they must automate because “everyone’s doing it,” out of fear that they will be undone by more up-to-date competitors (a paranoia encouraged by equipment vendors).** There is this vague belief that the drive to automate is inevitable, unavoidable, and this belief becomes a self-fulfilling prophesy. In the stampede, meanwhile, there is very little sober analysis of costs and benefits.[^3]

I think this is particularly noteworthy, because — as he further highlights — seldom did managers actually measure the efficiency of their factories or any kind of increase through the adoption of new technologies. On the contrary, even when measured, the effects were often small, if at all noticeable.[^4]  The economist Robert Solow once famously quipped: “You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.”[^5]

(I have also yet to see one “AI for efficiency” strategy paper that actually lays out measurable goals on what and how things are to be achieved.)

This should not be understood as a complete lack of efficiency (or productivity) gains through these tools but that this idea is primarily a *myth*. A story only thought to be true, because it is widely believed to be so.

## Productive vs. unproductive work — what‘s valued and what isn‘t

TTKTK Einleitung: Efficiency ist kein gutes Ziel, weil irreführend und priorisiert die falschen dinge

In the discussions around work, people like to distinguish between *productive* and *unproductive* tasks with the later being open for automation. Meetings are, for example, often seen as a necessary evil — thus often the first thing people try to optimize in whatever way. The same goes for the maintenance of existing tools, codebases or infrastructures — though it often accounts for 50–70 % of the work in software projects.[^6]

A recent example for this is a study by *Amazon*, noting that *AWS* developers are writing code for only hour a day on average (gasp!), using the other seven hours of the work day for “tedious, undifferentiated tasks such as learning codebases, writing and reviewing documentation, testing, managing deployments, troubleshooting issues or finding and fixing vulnerabilities”.[^7] 

Already you can see a clear distinction here between what *Amazon* itself sees as productive and unproductive tasks. Of course, this line of reasoning was used to sell an AI-powered assistant tool.

This distinction between *productive* and *unproductive* is mostly besides the point, though. Would a developer be more *productive* if he or she never went to meetings, never maintained or documented, but simply wrote as many lines of code as possible per day? If you‘d follow this simplistic line of reasoning the answer is a resounding *yes*. In reality, of course, you'd quickly have an unusable, undocumented code-base without any clear direction or function. While the individual productivity sky-rockets, the organization would come to a screeching halt.

The danger of this kind of thinking is perfectly encapsulated of what Eryk Salvaggio calls the “productivity myth” of AI:

> The importance and value of thinking about our work and why we do it is waved away as a distraction.**The goal of writing, this myth suggests, is filling a page rather than the process of thought that a completed page represents.**[^8]

This kind of thinking is unhelpful because it, again, lends itself to starting from a given technology. It is in effect the same argument as before. The distinction between *productive* and *unproductive tasks results from the underlying goal of *efficiency* through *automation* — there‘s no reflection on *why* this course of action is worth taking. Thus, every productivity gain can be, at best, classified as accidental.

## Automation realism

Let‘s also talk about some of the other side-effects automation might have. We have already seen that the “automation = efficiency”-argument isn‘t as clean cut as some might suggest. At least not when viewed from the perspective of workers.

For example during the 50s and 60s automation ment not increasingly plushy labor conditions but instead a speedup of the work, higher rates of accidents, and the emergence of *Taylorism*. Less workers were expected to fulfill a higher production quota, while being increasingly pressured, controlled, and measured by the management.

> Looking at the grand sweep of human history, increasing labor productivity won through technological and scientifc development has, paradoxically, not meant the reduction in the amount of work people generally do. Quite the opposite: innovation and development have led to both increased productivity and more work under unvaryingly hierarchical conditions.[^9]

The same dynamic plays out again today. A study by *upwork* noted that while 96% of C-level managers expected an increase of productivity through *AI*-tools, about 47% of employees had no idea how to fulfill these goals with 77% even saying that *AI* actually increased their workload.[^10]

Or the use of *AI* in medicine, which in some cases resulted in centralized decision-making through opaque algorithms, while increasing the control of the workers — effectively degrading not only working conditions but also the care given to patients.[^11]

There‘s a real danger that the adoption of automation might not mean spending more time on *productive* tasks instead of *unproductive* ones — but an increase of tasks in total, while degrading the work as a whole, cutting support (“slack”) out of the organisation and upping internal metrics.

It should also be noted that, so far, evidence for actual increases in the *productivity* of organizations through the adoption of *ai* are slim.

## Being effective, not efficient

So what might be a better way to think about the adoption of *ai*, especially in journalism? As I noted before, this is not an article against the adoption of such tools but more a plea for a thoughtful one.

The starting point should not be, as noted above, “how can *AI* make journalists more efficient?” — but instead start with a fundamental analysis, instead of starting with a tool:

- Which parts of my workflow are inefficient and why?
- What does need to be changed? (e.g. bottlenecks, slow devision making, lacking alignment, etc.)
- And after that: Which practices and tools fit this task best?

And generally, managers should not count on sudden increases of organizational productivity, while workers should not count on a lighter work-load.

There‘s also a second (in my opinion) more interesting question to ask:

- **How can the work be made more effective?**

So in the case of journalism this might mean asking: is the work done effective in truth-telling, public accountability, fostering informed citizenship, or producing high-quality journalism? And if not: which practices and tools can help?
