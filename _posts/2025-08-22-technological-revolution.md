I really dislike the term “technological revolution”. And with that I mean I loathe it. It‘s a cliché. It stops me from continuing to read whatever was written around it. It reeks of MBA, LinkedIn posts, and yet-another-startup-pitch-on-stage.

There’s a certain irony to it. At least historically speaking revolution (from the *Latin* *revolutio*, ‘a turn around’) it described a change in societal power relations. Though, it‘s more literal meaning—a turn around—fits the revolving parade of hyped up technologies way better.

As the historian David Edgerton has noted: “The technological boosterism of the past has too often been turned into the history of our material world.”[^1] What has been labeled a ‘technological revolution’ was often far from it.

Take the first technological revolution. We tend to imagine it as a sudden eruption of steam engines remaking society. But Edgerton shows the widespread use of those machines peaked much later, in the nineteenth rather than the eighteenth century. And as Raphael Samuel argued, the real driver of productivity was not the machine but the reorganization of labor: the factory, the disciplining of workers, the invention of ‘sweating’ as a management practice. In other words, the revolution was social, not technological.[^2]

‘Technological revolution’ is thus a pretty meaningless term as a lens for analysis, especially as it is used by the LinkedIn-breed of tech enthusiasts. 

But its use is as interesting as it is revealing. This kind of language presents a form of technological determinism — it assumes that radical change flows automatically from the machine itself. And once you frame change as inevitable, you push aside the real questions: who benefits, who loses, who decides? Call something a revolution and you can ironically skip the politics, the complexities, the mess of actual technological use. You never stop to ask yourself how the technology in question actually impacts power-relations. 

In the popular tech discourse, ‘revolution’ is almost always used to hype up boringly incremental improvement — faster processors, smarter algorithms, cheaper batteries — while still keeping intact the hierarchies of ownership and control. It's a branding tactic not a useful descriptor of actual systemic change.

Or to phrase it differently:

> It's only a technological revolution if it actually changes power relations; otherwise, it's just a sparkling reallocation of capital.


[^1]: Edgerton, D. (2006). ‘The Shock of the Old’. Profile Books Ltd.
[^2]: Samuel, R. (1977). ‘WORKSHOP OF THE WORLD: Steam Power and Hand Technology in Mid-Victorian Britain’. History Workshop Journal, Volume 3, Issue 1, Spring 1977, Pages 6–72.
