At the end of 2025 I decided to crawl 14 years[^2] of *NiemanLab* predictions with the goal of looking for long-term patterns of how the future discourse has changed over the years.[^1]

I ended up producing a 24pg. slide deck in german, which can be found [here](https://drive.google.com/file/d/17vKSGtxY2aslX__C069okIcDB_S-EEzm/view?usp=sharing). The following is a shortened english translation of it, with some additional notes.

Generally speaking I used the same methodology as *Google n-gram*, meaning I calculated up the frequencies in which words appeard per 1.000 words. Generally, I searched for word-groups. “VR”, for example, would mean searching for “VR” and “virtual reality”, as well as plural and genitiv forms of both terms.

## A word about the predictions

While authors are generally asked to write predictions about the next year, these pieces should be at best understood as op-eds. They generally reflect what their authors think will be important, often themselves driven by attention and hype-cycles. This analysis should thus be understood as an analysis of a sliver of the future-discourse in the industry and nothing more.

While the tonality of the articles thus tends to be optimistically cautious, they have over the years become more and more critical—most likely reflecting the change in the economic landscape journalism is operating in. With platforms and tech-companies adopting on increasingly apathetic or outright antagonistic strategies towards the industry. Notably, the frequency of hype-y wordings (“disruptive”, “revolution”, etc.) has remaind relatively stable over the years.

## Attention as an early indicator

At least in retrospect, it can be said that the low level of attention paid to topics such as *VR* and *crypto* was a good indicator of the low relevance of these two technology bundles. It is even worth, once you consider the push by *Facebook* and *Microsoft* towards the *Metaverse* with the term just barely making an appearance in the predictions.

## Social Media is losing it‘s relevance as a future topic

Social platforms remain central to journalism in practice, but are increasingly losing importance in the discourse on the future. One reason for this is the repeated disappointments and the increasingly obvious dependence of journalism.

Historically, Facebook and Twitter dominated the debate. Twitter served as an elite and agenda-setting medium, while Facebook was central to traffic generation, distribution, and monetisation as a mass medium.

## Tired: Reader, Wired: Community

Parallel to the decline in importance of social platforms, there has been a semantic shift among the description of the audiences of journalism: mentions of ‘community’ have grown by 456 %, while “readers” and ‘users’ are becoming less important.

As journalism has grown disillusioned with platform dependence, the language of community building has intensified. Whether this represents actual organizational change or aspirational rebranding is difficult to determine from prediction discourse alone. But the linguistic pattern suggests an attempt to internalize the social functions that platforms once promised to provide—to build direct relationships with audiences rather than rent access through algorithmic intermediaries. If social platforms are losing relevance as a topic of future discussion, it may be because journalism is attempting to absorb their logic rather than depend on their infrastructure.

This, in some way, mirrors the observation of journalism scholars how media companies increasingly copy strategies of big platforms, such as “personalisation” and data stratgies.[^5]

## Creators, so hot right now

If there‘s something that could be described as an emerging trend, it might be “creators”, even though the definition remains vague: authors understand creators to be a broad group of individuals who build an audience via social networks without necessarily being journalists.

Authors write about several different groups:

- *Influencers* (creators with a strong focus on reach and little journalistic interest)
- *News creators* (individuals who work in journalism in the broadest sense, but do not necessarily feel connected to journalism)
- *Hybrid creator-journalists* (journalists who build their own audiences via platforms)
- *Community-embedded creators* (individuals who place a strong focus on a networked community and become its voice)

These distinctions may matter more to journalism than to audiences, who often consume all four types interchangeably. But the subtext of the discourse is revealing: creators are consistently credited with achieving what journalism has struggled to do—building sustainable audience relationships and business models on the very platforms that have failed journalism.

This represents a curious inversion. In the late 2000s, the "blogosphere" was discussed as a complement or challenge to institutional journalism. Today's creator discourse carries a similar energy, but the power dynamics have shifted. Platform algorithms now favor individual creators over institutional outlets, and creators command audience loyalty that many news organizations envy.

Whether journalism should compete with, collaborate with, or learn from creators remains unresolved in the predictions. But the rising attention suggests the industry increasingly sees creators not as a separate category but as an alternative model for how journalistic work might be organized and sustained.

## AI as a brand and mirror

**58 % of all predictions mentioned “AI” in some form (+16 % YoY).** And although the discourse surrounding ‘AI’ in journalism is not new, it has exploded since the launch of ChatGPT in 2021.

Generally, the discourse surrounding “AI” can be split into two hype cycles. From 2016 to 2020, machine learning and smart speakers dominated, but were replaced by “generative AI“ and LLMs in the hype cycle after 2022. “AI” is thus less a technology and more a branding term for a wide variety of systems.

This mirrors the argument by the historian Thomas Haigh, that, from a historical perspective, ‘artificial intelligence’ should not be understood as a technology or group of technologies, but rather as a brand.[^3]

“AI” as a brand describes a range of methods and approaches that simulate human behaviour and are part of the larger project of “superintelligence.” Machine learning, for example, was explicitly not part of the field of research for decades because it was understood as pure statistics. This only changed when breakthroughs were made.[^4]

From a pragmatic point of view, AI investments, the adoption of AI tools and the launch of AI projects thus also serve the purpose of self-branding: they signal that companies and individuals are in tune with the times and innovative.

If “AI” is less a clearly defined technology than a collective term or brand, then predictions about AI in journalism should not be understood literally or technically. They rarely deal with the technology itself—its limits or its actual use—but remain speculative. In this sense, AI predictions in journalism say more about how authors want to reorganise, justify or protect journalistic work than about what AI can objectively achieve or will achieve in the future.
