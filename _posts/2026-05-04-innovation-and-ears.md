Histories of innovation often make the mistake of treating innovation as a historical constant, as if the idea itself were timeless, universal, and free of historical baggage. This is, of course, false—as I would otherwise not be writing this article. 
On the contrary, some of the historical ideas bound up with it—of thinking differently, of conspiracy, and of sudden violent change—are historical remnants that, if they remain unexamined, will lead to the failure of today's innovator. Especially the people who like to style themselves as such.

What follows is based on the works of the late Benoît Godin, especially his book Innovation Contested, published by Routledge.

The history of innovation does not begin with Schumpeter but in the third and fourth century.[^1] You might be surprise to learn that the ancient Romans did not use *innovare* or *innovo* but other terms, such as *novitas* or *res nova*, to describe novelties. *Innovare* would have its origin in Christian religious texts, to describe a spiritual "renewal" by means of introducing novelty.[^2] For the first centuries after its appearance, *innovation* would remain a almost exclusively a religious term, far removed from technological concerns.

In 1548, Edward VI, King of England, issued a proclamation.

{% include img-full.html path="2026-05-04-proclamation.svg" info="The full title: A proclamation against those that doeth innouate, alter or leaue doune any rite or ceremonie in the Church, of their priuate aucthoritie: and against them which preacheth without licence, set furth the .vj. daie of Februarij, in the seconde yere of the Kynges Maiesties most gracious reigne."%}

This has to be understood in the context of sixteenth-century England. Edward's father and predecessor, Henry VIII, had in 1527 broken with the Roman Catholic Church to establish the Anglican Church along Protestant doctrines. Innovation had suddenly become political.
