The use of AI in companies is regularly plagued by a paradox: Why do the promised efficiency gains fail to materialize or fall short of expectations despite high investments and promises to the contrary?[^1]

The simple, albeit harsh, answer is that while automation takes over tasks previously performed by humans, it simultaneously creates new ones—a phenomenon known in academic literature as the *substitution fallacy*. Even worse, a work process as a whole can only move as fast as its slowest component; often, AI merely shifts the bottleneck from the creation of content to its verification and oversight.

Coding works as a good example of the substitution fallacy: while the production of code has been accelerated, it has shifted the workload [onto debugging](https://www.gitclear.com/write_only_mode_ai_research) [and maintenance](https://leaddev.com/technical-direction/how-ai-generated-code-accelerates-technical-debt). There have been promises of developers moving into de-facto software architecture roles, but in practice this granting of power over decision-making is often limited to a small subset of engineers. Similarly, in newsrooms these same patterns of automation and human oversight emerge. Journalists are often expected to verify and correct AI-generated content, adding layers of responsibility that were not present before the introduction of these systems. Here one frequently encounters the *human-in-the-loop* model, where a person serves as the final authority to verify the accuracy of output generated by automated content systems. 

Though, what is often described as *human-in-the-loop* is actually a *human-on-the-loop* configuration, where the human supervises and intervenes only when necessary, rather than being an integral part of the decision-making process. In fact, journalists are in some instances explicitly excluded from the core machine decision-making, reducing their role to that of a safety net rather than a true collaborator.

As psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge observed as early as 1983,[^3] the human role in such configurations becomes actually more critical than it was before automation—an irony rarely considered by the developers of these systems. As the designers’ goals are often the replacement of human labour with automated systems, they inadvertently create pitfalls that make human oversight not just necessary, but increasingly demanding, if not impossible.

## Fatigue, deskilling, and fragility

The longer humans spend continuously monitoring automated systems, the less attention they pay to individual outputs—increasing the likelihood that incorrect information will be published. “Even when you try to carefully check everything every single day, things get overlooked. If 90 percent is correct, the errors often go unnoticed. Yet, the mistakes that do slip through are frequently the most embarrassing ones,” explained an editorial manager interviewed for a [recent research project](https://www.media-lab.de/de/research/status-quo-ki-in-den-medien/). And oversight is necessary, because hallucinations are ultimately a consequence of the basic functionality of large language models—they simply cannot be eliminated entirely.[^4] 

At the same time, humans lose the skills required for tasks that are now handled by automation; they become *deskilled*. This effect has been known and well-researched among pilots for decades: the less practice they get—due to heavy reliance on autopilots—the less capable they are of intervening during an emergency. Such emergencies become particularly critical when automated systems fail suddenly, without any warning or feedback.

Between 2018 and 2019, automated systems were a contributing factor in two crashes involving the Boeing 737 Max series, in which more than 300 people lost their lives. The issue stemmed from a faulty sensor that triggered an unnecessary emergency response within the flight control system, causing the aircraft to pitch downward. Lacking information about what had occurred or how to disable the system, the pilots struggled against this background automation until both planes ultimately crashed.

Automated systems are often fragile when they encounter situations outside their design specifications. Humans must be capable not only of catching these issues but, ideally, of anticipating them—which requires a deep understanding of the machine, often only gained through experience.[^5] While the impact in the field of journalism may be less immediate or catastrophic, errors here can still carry serious personal and legal consequences.

Just putting humans in the loop is not enough. If the system is designed in a way that makes human oversight excessively demanding or practically impossible, the intended safety net becomes ineffective, leading to the system eating the promised efficiency gains.[^9]

## Old findings, still relevant

It is worth emphasising that these effects have been **well-documented and studied for decades**, yet they continue to be overlooked in the design, deployment, and discussion of automated systems. 

Just take a look at this table from a 2013(!) paper, titled aptly “The Seven Deadly Myths of ‘Autonomous Systems’”, which is more grounded than most of the contemporary think-pieces on automation and AI.[^6]


| Putative Benefit [of autonomous systems]                                                        | Real Complexity                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Increased performance is obtained from substitution (machine activity replaces human activity). | Practice is transformed; roles change; familiar features and even valued habits are altered—creating a different, often problematic, envisioned world.          |
| Frees up humans by offloading work to machines.                                                 | Creates new cognitive work for humans, often at the wrong times; gains are used to demand more, faster, or more complex work—the law of stretched systems.      |
| Frees up limited attention by focusing people on the correct answer.                            | Creates more threads to track; makes it harder to stay aware of and integrate activities, increasing coordination costs continuously.                           |
| Less human knowledge is required.                                                               | New knowledge and skills are required; people may lose sufficient context to make decisions after being removed from the loop—automation surprise.              |
| Agent functions autonomously.                                                                   | Success depends on teamwork with people and other agents—principles of interdependence.                                                                         |
| Same feedback to humans will be sufficient.                                                     | New types and levels of feedback are needed to support evolving roles, adding continuous coordination costs.                                                    |
| Agent enables more system flexibility in a general way.                                         | Explosion of features, options, and modes introduces new demands, error types, and failure paths—automation surprises.                                          |
| Human errors are reduced.                                                                       | Both humans and agents remain fallible; coordination breakdowns emerge, and agents can obscure information needed for human decisions—principles of complexity. |

## Teamwork, not gap-filling

This does not mean that efficiency gains through automation are impossible. The question, however, is how precisely said automation is designed. More than 40 years ago, Bainbridge observed that humans are often deployed merely as gap-fillers to compensate for the shortcomings of machines. The result is jobs consisting of disjointed, residual tasks—the fragments that could not be automated.

In the decades that followed, research fields—partly inspired by Bainbridge’s work—explored how collaboration between humans and machines could be structured to be truly productive. A key insight is that both components should be viewed as *joint cognitive systems*—a unified system that must be designed and evaluated as a whole.[^7] Still, you can see people weighing human skills versus machine capabilities, rather than considering how the two can complement each other within a cohesive system. The AI industry in particular has gotten a lot of mileage out of framing AI as a replacement and threat to human labour rather than as a collaborator.

It is insufficient to simply attempt to compensate for a machine’s limitations through human labour if those very limitations are potentially exacerbated by the human operator. A *human-in-the-loop* must possess genuine control; the machine’s processes must remain transparent and observable; and errors must be both anticipatable and comprehensible.

Furthermore, efficiency is often not the optimal goal for automation. In the context of knowledge work, in particular, effectiveness is frequently more important. If new tools enable journalists to become more effective in their research or in the production of content, or in interactions with readers? Again, there have been a lot of promises about journalists being empowered—even freed from mundane tasks—but similarly to the situation in software development, this clashes with the realities of how these tools are actually integrated into workflows. Efficiency (or higher production quotas) is often mandated alongside AI usage, not an observed result of it.[^8] This is the same dynamic as with the developers who were promised architect roles noted above.

The fact that decades of research into automation have seemingly had no influence on the discourse surrounding AI is irritating, yet hardly surprising. The rhetoric that frames new technologies as a revolution and a historical rupture implies that the insights of the past are obsolete—regardless of whether this is actually true. Instead, we will spend the next years re-discovering lessons that have been known for a long time.

Before a newsroom puts a person in a loop, it should be able to answer three questions: can they actually override the system, can they see and understand what it did, and can they predict how it fails?
