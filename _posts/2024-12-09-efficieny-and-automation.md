Again and again I encounter a particular line reasoning for the adoption of "*AI*"[^1] in journalism: it will make journalists more *efficient*. I am not going to argue for or against the adoption of of AI in journalism but I want to investigate this argument for a bit.

Let's start with the obvious part: given you'd want to increase the efficiency of a newsroom, would you start by adopting AI tools? Yes, this is a rather cheap rhetorical trick I am playing here but an important one, because what are we (or you) trying to achieve? Is the goal the adoption of AI-tools or the increasing of efficiency?

There are of course multiple different strategies for an increase of efficiency in an organization: better decision-making, increasing work speed, optimizing work-flows and — yes — adopting new tools. But framing the question around *efficiency* not AI-use gives you very a different menu of things to choose from.

## Old reasons, old myths

I do think most people using the "*AI* for efficiency gains"-argument are somewhat honest in this regard. The goal is not primarily an efficiency gain but tool adoption, often combined with a notion of inevitability. There's no other option if you want to stay competitive, media companies **have** to adopt or risk being left behind.

David Noble, one of the most prominent historians of automations, encountered a similar line of reasoning when it came to factory automation in the 70s and 80s:

> ==Managers feel they must automate because “everyone’s doing it,” out of fear that they will be undone by more up-to-date competitors (a paranoia encouraged by equipment vendors).== There is this vague belief that the drive to automate is inevitable, unavoidable, and this belief becomes a self-fulfilling prophesy. In the stampede, meanwhile, there is very little sober analysis of costs and benefits.[^2]

I think this is particularly noteworthy, because — as he further highlights — seldom did managers actually measure the efficiency of their factories or any kind of increase through the adoption of new technologies. On the contrary, even when measured, the effects were often small, if at all noticeable.[^3]  The economist Robert Solow once famously quipped: “You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.”[^4]

This should not be understood as a complete lack of efficiency(or productivity) gains but more that this idea or "automation = efficiency increase" constitutes a *myth*. A story only true, because it is widely believed to be so, not because it is actually true. The reason for this is that the effect of automation, especially in complex social organizations is seldom easily identifiable.

## Productive vs. unproductive work — what‘s valued and what isn‘t

In the discussions around work processes, people like to distinguish between *productive* and *unproductive* tasks with the later being open for automation. Meetings are, for example, often seen as a necessary evil — thus often the first thing people try to optimize in whatever way. The same goes for the maintenance of existing tools, codebases or infrastructures — though it often accounts for 50–70 % of the work in software projects.[^5]

A recent example for this is a study by *Amazon*, noting that *AWS* developers are writing code for only hour a day on average (gasp!), using the other seven hours of the work day for “tedious, undifferentiated tasks such as learning codebases, writing and reviewing documentation, testing, managing deployments, troubleshooting issues or finding and fixing vulnerabilities”.[^6] 

Already you can see a clear distinction here between what *Amazon* itself sees as productive and unproductive tasks. Of course, this line of reasoning was used to sell an AI-powered assistant tool.

This distinction between *productive* and *unproductive* is mostly besides the point, though. Would a developer be more *productive* if he or she never went to meetings, never maintained or documented, but simply wrote as many lines of code as possible per day? If you‘d follow this simplistic line of reasoning the answer is a resounding yes. In reality, of course, you'd quickly have an unusable, undocumented code-base without any clear direction or function. While the individual productivity sky-rockets, the organization would come to a screeching halt.

The danger of this kind of thinking is perfectly encapsulated of what Eryk Salvaggio calls the “productivity myth”:

> The importance and value of thinking about our work and why we do it is waved away as a distraction. ==The goal of writing, this myth suggests, is filling a page rather than the process of thought that a completed page represents.==[^7]

This kind of thinking is unhelpful because it, again, lends itself to starting from technology. It is in effect the same argument as before. The distinction between *productive* and *unproductive tasks results from the underlying goal of *efficiency* through *automation* — there‘s no reflection of *why* this course of action is worth taking. Thus, every productivity gain can be, at best, classified as accidental.

## Automation realism

Let‘s also talk about some of the other side-effects automation might have. We have already seen that the “automation = efficiency”-argument isn‘t as clean cut as some might suggest.

For example during the 50s and 60s automation at the plants of the *Ford Automotive company* ment not increasingly plushy labor conditions but instead a speedup of the work and the emergence of *taylorism*. Less workers were expected to fulfill a higher quota, while being increasingly controlled and measured by the management.

> Looking at the grand sweep of human history, increasing labor productivity won through technological and scientifc development has, paradoxically, not meant the reduction in the amount of work people generally do. Quite the opposite: innovation and development have led to both increased productivity and more work under unvaryingly hierarchical conditions.[^8]

The same dynamic plays out again today. A study by *upwork* noted that while 96% of C-level managers expected an increase of productivity through *AI*-tools, about 47% of employees had no idea how to fulfill these goals with 77% even saying that *AI* actually increased their workload.[^9]

## Being effective

So what might be a better way to think about the adoption of *ai*, especially in journalism? As I noted before, this is not an article against the adoption of such tools but more a plea for a thoughtful adoption.

**The core goal of technology adoption should not be a gain in *efficiency* or even *productivity* but an increase in *effectiveness*.**
