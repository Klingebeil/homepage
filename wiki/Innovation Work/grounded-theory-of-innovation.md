# Grounded Theory of Innovation in Journalism

This is still a bit rough but the general idea is to sketch out an alternative approach to *innovation* then the dominant image presented by the tech-industry—which is still dominant in the media companies.

Innovation is often shaped less by what’s “new” and more by what’s already there—infrastructure, habits, workarounds, and constraints. A grounded approach respects and build on this, while dominant narratives often ignore or erase it.

1. Incremental and Adaptive Change
	- Innovation is understood as a _slow, iterative process_ shaped by constraints like shrinking budgets, legacy systems, or editorial norms. These are understood as part of the territory, not organisational resistance.
	- It further recognizes, that change often occurs through _tinkering_, *improvisation*, or *workarounds* rather than through disruptive "breakthroughs."
2. Technology-in-Use over speculative Tech Potential
	- What matters is _how technology is actually used_, not what it could theoretically do.
	- E.g. Newsrooms often repurpose tools like Excel, WhatsApp, or Google Docs in creative, journalistic ways.
	- The _invisible infrastructure_—CMS quirks, editorial workflows, legacy IT—often drives or constrains innovation more than any shiny new tool fresh out the valley.
3. Context-Specific Problem Solving
	- Innovations are driven by _local newsroom needs_, not abstract notions of scalability or disruption or even technological trends.
	- For example, a small newsroom in a rural area might develop a new comparatively low-tech SMS-based reporting system—an innovation rooted in its own context, not in universal tech principles.
4. Cultural and Ethical Dimensions
	- Innovation would be understood not just as a technical or business challenge but as an _ethical and epistemological_ one: How do new tools and formats affect trust, verification, and public service journalism?
5. Power, Labor, and Identity
	- A grounded approach would surface the _power dynamics_ around innovation—who gets to define it, who gets funding, whose labor supports it (often women, freelancers, or junior staff), and who gets credit.        
6. Resistance and Non-Adoption
	- Such an approach might also examine _resistance to innovation_ as a rational and meaningful choice, rather than framing it as backwardness or inertia.
