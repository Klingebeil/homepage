# Grounded Theory of Innovation in Journalism

Innovation in journalism is often framed as something brought in from the outside—a disruptive technology, a consulting firm’s playbook, or a Silicon Valley ‘solution.’ But what if it’s already there, hidden in the workarounds of a reporter tweaking a CMS hack, the ad-hoc collaborations of a small newsroom, or the quiet refusal to adopt tools that don’t fit? This is a case for recognizing that these outside methods are often itself the problem and an argument for grounded innovation: not importing change, but cultivating it from the soil up.

The basic idea here is that there’s no such thing as universal approaches to innovation.  

A grounded approach starts from the material realities of journalism—its tools, constraints, and labor. Innovation here is shaped less by what’s “new” and more by what’s already there: infrastructure, habits, and the friction of real-world use. A grounded approach respects and builds on these, while dominant narratives often ignore or erase it. At worst they redefine it as “resistance” or “ignorance”, which will inevitably lead to antagonism between innovation actors and their environment, disangegament, and possibly burn out for the people tasked with enforcing outside ideas.


The following is an example of how *innovation* might be redefined into a grounded approach by emphasizing and reinterpreting its features and goals when compared to the usual tech-industry approach.

I want to emphasize that this is not a new idea or even approach—this is how the industry has in fact always innovated. Yet today, innovation is often framed as a break from history not a continuation—a ‘disruption’ imposed by outside logic. The true danger lies not in missing a given new technology but in ignoring this history in favor of the idea that imitating the tech-industry might resolve the economic, technical, and societal challenges the industry faces. 

This is an argument for rediscovering journalism’s long tradition of contextual adaptation, where progress is shaped by possibilites and constraints in equal parts.

---

1. **Incremental and Adaptive Change**
	- Innovation is understood as a *slow*, *iterative process shaped by constraints* like shrinking budgets, legacy systems, or editorial norms. These are understood as part of the territory, not organisational resistance.
	- It further recognizes, that change often occurs through *tinkering*, *improvisation*, or *workarounds* rather than through disruptive "breakthroughs."
2. **Technology-in-Use over speculative Tech Potential**
	- What matters is *how technology is actually used*, not what it could theoretically do.
	- E.g. Newsrooms often repurpose tools like Excel, WhatsApp, or Google Docs in creative, journalistic ways.
	- The invisible infrastructure—CMS quirks, editorial workflows, legacy IT—often drives or constrains innovation more than any shiny new tool fresh out the valley.
	- Note: While the tech-industry can work on hype to generate investments, the economics of the media industry seldom allow for this. Marketing should not be confused with prediction.
3. **Context-Specific Problem Solving**
	- Innovations are driven by *local newsroom needs*, not abstract notions of scalability or disruption or even technological trends.
	- For example, a small newsroom in a rural area might develop a new comparatively low-tech SMS-based reporting system—an innovation rooted in its own context, not in universal tech principles.
4. **The innovator as a steward not a heroic figure**
	- The innovator's role is to *nurture* existing practices, tend to organizational ecosystems, and create conditions where improvements can organically develop.
	- This stewardship values patience, observation, and contextual understanding over rapid transformation or universal solutions.
5. **Cultural and Ethical Dimensions**
	- Innovation would be understood not just as a technical or business challenge but as an *ethical* and *epistemological* one: How do new tools and formats affect trust, verification, and public service journalism?
6. **Power, Labor, and Identity**
	- A grounded approach would surface the *power dynamics* around innovation—who gets to define it, who gets funding, whose labor supports it (often women, freelancers, or junior staff), and who gets credit.        
7. **Resistance and Non-Adoption**
	- Such an approach might also examine *resistance to innovation* as a rational and meaningful choice, rather than framing it as backwardness or inertia.
8. **Audiences as Actors, Not "Users"**
	- Dominant innovation models treat audiences as abstractions (e.g., engagement metrics, personas) or problems to solve (e.g., "how do we get millennials to pay?"). The ‘user’ is not a design problem but a lived reality.
	- Instead of treating audiences as passive "users" to be captured or optimized (via metrics, paywalls, or gamification), a grounded approach starts from and recognizes how audiences already engage with journalism—materially, socially, and ethically. It doesn’t assume audiences need fixing but asks how journalism can align with their lived realities.
