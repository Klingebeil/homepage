# Why AI chat is a terrible interace for news

## Loss of Context and Depth
Chat interfaces simplify complex news, stripping away essential context. When, for example, a significant global conflict is reduced to “key takeaways” or “top 5 facts,” we lose geopolitical history, conflicting perspectives, and systemic factors. 

The medium prioritizes brevity over comprehensiveness, leading to what scholars call *epistemic narrowing* — where our understanding becomes increasingly limited by the format itself.

## Hidden Decision-Making
The chat interface conceals how news is selected and prioritized. Users can't easily determine if summaries are biased, why certain sources were excluded, or whether corporate interests shaped the information presented. This “black-boxing” - hiding complex systems behind simple interfaces - undermines transparency and accountability in news delivery, values essential to democratic discourse.

## Discouraging Critical Engagement
Chat interfaces optimize for convenience rather than reflection. News becomes a stream of quick facts rather than material for deep consideration. A well designed newspaper front page can use imagery and layout to convey the gravity of a humanitarian crisis, while a chatbot might reduce this to a one-sentence update. 

The design prioritizes smooth efficiency over the friction that often triggers critical thinking.

## Eroding Collective Discourse
News consumption has traditionally been partly public and social. Chat interfaces push us toward private, individualized consumption that's customized and ephemeral. This shift hampers shared discourse, making it harder to establish common ground for civic dialogue and political action. As our news experiences diverge, filter bubbles deepen and collective understanding diminishes.

## The Constructed “User”
When companies claim “users want news in chat form,” they often rely on engagement metrics that ignore qualitative dimensions of experience. This treats people as market actors seeking convenience rather than citizens requiring rich information. We should ask: Who is this "user"? Are they critical thinkers or passive consumers? 

Do they genuinely prefer this format, or is it simply what's being offered?

## Co-Production of Behavior
Technologies and users shape each other. A reader who once engaged with long-form journalism might adapt to expecting only brief summaries. Their attention span, expectations, and trust in journalism are reconfigured by the interface. Saying “users want this” is ultimately like saying “fish want smaller tanks” — without acknowledging how the environment itself shapes desires and capabilities.

Rather than simply accepting AI chat as the future of news delivery, we should critically examine what's lost when we reduce journalism to a convenient conversation. Better alternatives might integrate the accessibility of chat with the depth, transparency, and collective experience that traditional news formats can provide.
