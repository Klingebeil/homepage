# Why AI chat is a terrible interace for news

## Sociotechnical Mediation and Loss of Context
Technologies are not neutral; they mediate social, political, and epistemic relationships. In a chat interface, the conversational AI (or agent) shapes not just what news is consumed but how it is framed, prioritized, and emotionally conveyed.

**Issue**{: .label .color-red} A chat interface tends to personalize and simplify news, potentially leading to:
- Loss of context: Background information, conflicting sources, and broader systemic factors may be omitted.
- Epistemic narrowing: Users may receive filtered or truncated versions of complex stories due to space or style constraints.

**Example**{: .label .color-green} A war reported via chat might focus on “key takeaways” or “top 5 facts,” omitting the geopolitical history, local perspectives, or contested narratives.

## Black-boxing and Algorithmic Opacity
STS critiques the “black-boxing” of technology—when complex systems are made to seem simple and trustworthy, hiding the power dynamics and design choices embedded in them.

**Issue**{: .label .color-red} The chat interface may conceal how news is selected, prioritized, or reworded:
- Is the summary biased?
- Was a particular source excluded due to an algorithmic ranking?
- Are corporate interests or state censorship shaping what the chat says?

This undermines transparency and accountability in news consumption, a core democratic value.

## Critical Design Lens: Flattening of Aesthetic and Critical Engagement
Good news design should provoke reflection, critical awareness, and challenge dominant assumptions—not just provide smooth, efficient experiences.

**Issue**{: .label .color-red} Chat interfaces are optimized for convenience and fluency. They discourage slow, critical engagement with news.
- News becomes a stream of "tidbits" rather than a space for reflection or dissent.
- Users are discouraged from exploring multiple perspectives or visual/aesthetic representations (e.g., photos, layouts, infographics) that traditional media offers.

**Example**{: .label .color-green} A well-designed newspaper layout can highlight the gravity of a humanitarian crisis through imagery and spatial prominence. A chat bot response might reduce this to a one-sentence update.

## Erosion of Publicness and Collective Reading (STS + Critical Design)
News consumption is a public, social activity. Chat interfaces reinforce individualized consumption—private, customized, ephemeral. This shift discourages shared discourse, diminishing the communal aspects of civic life.

Consequences:
- Less deliberative democracy.
- Harder to establish common ground for political action.
- Filter bubbles deepen because chats are one-on-one and adaptive.

## The "User" is a Construct, not a Fact
Categories like “the user” are not discovered, but constructed—by designers, marketers, engineers, and platforms. They are shaped by:

- Market research biases,
- A desire for efficiency and scalability,
- Embedded ideologies about convenience and value.

Claiming “users want news in chat form” is often based on quantitative data (e.g., engagement metrics) that ignore the qualitative, ethical, or political dimensions of experience.

Critical Questions:
- Who is this “user”? Are they critical thinkers or passive consumers?
- Do they want this format, or is it the only one being offered?
- Does high engagement mean informed consent or meaningful satisfaction?

The answer often reveals a neoliberal logic: people are treated as market actors, not as citizens with public responsibilities.

## The Reduction of the User to Preferences
Design shaped by user preference often treats convenience and speed as universal values. But this ignores:

- Cognitive depth,
- Aesthetic richness,
- Political complexity.

Critique: When designers say “this is what the user wants,” they often mean:

> This is what the user will click on fastest and most often.

This instrumentalizes the user, flattening human experience into behavioral metrics.

## Co-production of the Interface and the User
STS emphasizes that technologies and users co-produce each other. A chat interface doesn't just respond to a user—it shapes who the user becomes.

**Example**{: .label .color-green} A user who previously read long-form investigative journalism might, over time, adapt to expecting short summaries in a chat format.

Their expectations, attention span, and even trust in journalism are reconfigured by the interface.

So saying “users want this” is like saying “fish want smaller tanks”—without asking whether the tank is shrinking their world.
